Triple test, adrift: On the ruling on definition of ‘industry’

The Supreme Court's decision on industrial disputes risks undermining crucial labour protections and legislative intent.
📅 August 24, 2026  |  ⏱ 3 min read Daily Editorial Reader

On August 20, a nine-judge Bench of the Supreme Court of India delivered a judgment on the correctness of the ruling in Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board vs A. Rajappa (BWSSB) (1978). In that judgment, Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer had laid down1 what was called the “Triple Test” on what counts as an “industry” under Section 2(j) of the Industrial Disputes (ID) Act, 1947 (ID Act). Three conditions were to be met for an industry — a systematic activity, employer-employee cooperation, and production/distribution of goods and services to satisfy human wants other than those that are purely religious or spiritual. Profit motive2 was irrelevant3; what mattered in this determination was the nature of the activity, and only “sovereign functions” stood outside it. A five-judge Bench in State of U.P. vs Jai Bir Singh (2005) raised a doubt about this definition and a seven-judge Bench sent it to the current nine-judge Bench. This Bench, led by Chief Justice of India Surya Kant, left the Triple Test standing for all pending disputes under the older ID Act, which was repealed4 on November 21, 2025 when the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (IRC) came into force5. However, a majority of the nine-judge Bench also ruled that BWSSB will not be a “sheet anchor6” for interpreting Section 2(p) of the IRC. That is a mistake. In her dissenting opinion7, Justice B.V. Nagarathna held that the reference itself was unnecessary and that the Triple Test requires no interference — a view shared by three other judges. Her reasoning is compelling8. Since 1978, and particularly after the liberalisation9 and privatisation reforms of 1991, a far higher number of workers have moved to the private sector, out of the security of public employment. An expansive10 definition of industry, as entailed in11 the Triple Test, is therefore even more necessary now as a bulwark12 for the worker.

Yet, Section 2(p) of the IRC does not move away from13 Justice Krishna Iyer’s Triple Test formula in BWSSB and reproduces much of its essence. It is therefore difficult to understand why BWSSB has to be set aside14 when Section 2(p) itself comes up for interpretation. The Triple Test was also not merely a pro-labour device. An expansive10 definition of industry brought with it not just the ID Act’s protections but also its restrictions — for instance, a regulated route to retrenchment15 and closure for the employer, and a bar on workers striking at will16. In essence17, the Triple Test allowed for industrial peace rather than worker welfare alone. Severing18 that principle from the IRC, as the majority has done, cuts away19 the interpretive framework that allowed such disputes to be resolved. With the essence of the Triple Test carried into Section 2(p), it is now incumbent upon20 courts and tribunals to ensure that a change of statute is not read as a change of intent even if the majority judgment has removed the anchor to do so.

Core concerns: On the Indian economy, the road ahead

Sluggish industrial core growth signals upcoming economic headwinds and softening domestic consumer demand.
📅 August 24, 2026  |  ⏱ 2 min read Daily Editorial Reader

The slowdown in the growth of India’s core industrial sectors in July was not surprising, with other indicators predicting easing1 demand conditions as well. Growth in the index of core industries (ICI) slowed to 5.4% in July from 6% in the previous month. This was in keeping with2 the Manufacturing Purchasing Managers’ Index, which in July eased to its lowest level since August 2021 due to weak domestic demand conditions. While July’s growth in the ICI was the second-highest growth rate in the last seven months, the data nevertheless highlight some concerning aspects of the Indian economy. The first has to do with the fact that a large part of even this slower growth seems to be based on a statistical low base effect3. The coal sector, for example, grew at an 11-month high of 7.6% in July, but this was based on a contraction of 12.3% in July of last year. Similarly, the refinery products sector snapped4 a three-month streak of contraction to grow at 2.7%, but this too was based on a contraction in July 2025. The iron ore sector, however, grew at a very robust5 29.5%, albeit6 slower than a blistering7 44.5% in June. This seems heartening8 regardless of the contractions of 16.4% and 7.1% in June and July of last year, respectively. But the steel sector slowed drastically9 in July to 2.9% from 5.6% in June and 15.7% in July of last year.

The domestic crude oil and natural gas sectors have been lasting drags on the economy. Both have contracted continuously for at least the last 14 months for which the new series of the ICI has data. India’s crude oil imports rose 13.3% in volume terms in July, while Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) imports grew a more marginal11 1.5%, showing that the economy’s appetite is being whetted from abroad. This external dependence13 is proving very expensive. High oil prices have meant that the crude oil import bill jumped 41% in July. The 100% tariffs that the United States is preparing to levy on14 countries such as India that import Russian oil will once again burden Indian exporters. Moving to 20% ethanol-blending has not yet impacted oil imports materially15. Within the ICI, the only two bright spots of the economy were the cement and electricity sectors. The electricity sector grew at a robust5 9% in July, although this was slower than the two consecutive months of double-digit growth in May and June thanks to the prevalent17 heatwave conditions in many parts of the country. The cement sector sped up to 13.1%, a welcome acceleration. Such positive trends were few and far between18. Overall, the Indian economy looks to be in for a period of slack19 demand, higher costs, and moderating20 growth.

To build Delhi’s future, learn from past missteps

Delhi’s latest Master Plan must overcome bureaucratic inertia and agency fragmentation to succeed.
📅 August 24, 2026  |  ⏱ 2 min read Daily Editorial Reader

About 25 years ago, when Delhi’s third Master Plan came into effect, the city’s population was about 14 million, Gurugram and Noida were fledgling2 suburbs, the first lines of the capital’s Metro network were being laid, and a CNG policy for public transport had been introduced as a pollution-control measure. Since then, another 10 million people have made the capital their home, civic infrastructure of its neighbouring cities has come under acute3 stress, and the improvement in air quality that Delhi experienced for much of the 2000s and early 2010s has given way to an almost intractable5 pollution crisis. Delhi’s fourth Master Plan, notified last week, acknowledges these challenges. Its emphasis on expanding public transport, ecological restoration6, redevelopment, affordable housing and mixed land use is welcome.

One of the plan’s strengths is its recognition that Delhi cannot be built simply by expanding outward. With limited land available within the National Capital Territory, better utilisation7 of existing urban land will become more important. The proposed increase in housing supply, including an ambition of roughly 40 lakh additional homes by 2047 at affordable rates, and greater flexibility in development norms to permit higher densities is, therefore, significant. The plan also recognises the futility8 of rigidly demarcating9 residential and commercial areas. If implemented well, these measures could ease the city’s longstanding traffic congestion and improve air quality. Mixed land use, environment-friendly transport, the redevelopment and nurturing10 of green spaces, and greater attention to the city’s water bodies could all contribute to11 improving Delhi’s ecological health and enhance the well-being of its residents.

None of these ideas will succeed, however, without confronting the national capital’s most persistent governance problems: Municipal inertia12 and a lack of alignment13 among civic agencies, Centre and Delhi government. These deficits were the biggest bottleneck14 in realising the previous Master Plan’s vision of a more sustainable city. Over the past 25 years, research on pollution, Yamuna restoration6, waste management, urban flooding and transport development has pointed to the need for concerted15 action among departments. Metro expansion, for instance, works best when bus routes, last-mile connectivity16, parking rules and pedestrian facilities are designed around it. Land-use planning and infrastructure development cannot be divorced from17 the requirements of curbing18 water logging, while building approvals, fire safety and emergency response require effective information-sharing among construction, fire and municipal authorities. Repeated fire incidents, building collapses and the strain placed on the city’s infrastructure during heavy rainfall have exposed the risks of fragmented20 and overlapping responsibilities among its agencies. Delhi has a blueprint21 for improving liveability22. It needs to learn from past failures.

Fixed Prepositions

Contribute to (में योगदान देना): To help bring about, cause, or advance a specific outcome or condition.
Divorced from (से पूरी तरह अलग या विमुख): Completely separated, disconnected, or isolated from something.
Strain on (पर भारी दबाव या तनाव): Severe pressure, demand, or tension exerted upon an existing resource or system.

Phrasal Verbs

Come into effect (लागू होना): To begin to apply or take official legal force.
Give way to (का स्थान किसी और चीज़ द्वारा ले लिया जाना): To be replaced, superseded, or followed by something else.

One-Word Substitutions

Fledgling (नया और अनुभवहीन): An entity, settlement, or enterprise that is young, newly developed, and inexperienced.
Restoration (पुनर्स्थापना): The action of returning something to a former, healthy, or unimpaired condition.
Futility (निरर्थकता): The quality of being completely pointlessness or incapable of producing any useful result.
Inertia (जड़ता या अकर्मण्यता): A tendency to do nothing or to remain unchanged; sluggishness in administrative action.
Bottleneck (अवरोध या अड़चन): A point of congestion, constriction, or blockage that severely impedes progress.
Blueprint (विस्तृत रूपरेखा या योजना): A comprehensive design, technical drawing, or systematic roadmap for future execution.

Idioms & Phrases

Last-mile connectivity (अंतिम छोर तक संपर्क सुविधा): The final stage of a transit network delivering passengers directly to their ultimate destinations.

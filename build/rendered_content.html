Danger from above: On the Nepal flood

“
Himalayan disaster risks require robust infrastructure regulations, satellite tracking, and proactive governance.
📅 August 28, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

Disaster struck Nepal on Wednesday after a flash flood ravaged1 the Himalayas and foothills. As of Thursday, at least 389 people including several foreign tourists, had died, while at least 300 Indians remained missing. Satellite imagery analysed by global agencies, including the Indian Space Research Organisation, points to a glacier collapse or ice-rock avalanche in Tibet, possibly followed by a temporary river blockage and its sudden failure. An earthquake was initially suspected, but evidence has not borne it out2. While the immediate flood wave has passed, there are warnings of possible secondary flooding3 from debris blockages upstream. There has been extensive damage along the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli corridors, with settlements washed away and hydropower and monitoring infrastructure damaged. Nepalese authorities said 35 motorable and 45 suspension bridges and about 40 kilometres of roads had been damaged.

Given that it is the monsoon, north India and the larger Himalayan regions are under the influence of4 a powerful Western Disturbance. It would thus not have been unreasonable to attribute the floods to these extratropical5 storms that can cause clouds to collide with mountains and release enormous quantities of rain at short notice. But the ubiquity6 of satellites is helping establish a more complex picture, pointing to collapsing glaciers and recalling the October 2023 South Lhonak Lake disaster in Sikkim. There, an eponymous7 Himalayan glacier suddenly gave way8, releasing millions of tonnes of water, debris and rock that pulverised9 Sikkim’s largest hydropower project. Subsequent analysis showed that the resulting Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF) had been years in the making10. The Indian Himalayas have nearly 7,500 glacial lakes and about 15,000 glaciers, some monitored through remote sensing11. Yet, fully assessing their condition requires site visits, difficult because these lakes lie in inhospitable12 terrain while expeditions are generally feasible13 only from July to September. Even with satellite monitoring, accurately forecasting a glacier collapse remains exceedingly14 difficult — and can be counterproductive15 if warnings trigger panic among residents who, often because of habit or lack of alternatives, are unlikely to move out of harm’s way. Dams and hydropower projects in such terrain are often among the first casualties17 of these natural outbursts, which are increasing amid global warming. Beyond relief and rescue, governments must enforce construction codes, discourage settlements and large projects in known vulnerable zones18, and invest in better tracking of glacier health. Such monitoring can feed long-term disaster planning into development programmes. A tragedy is frequently not the result of a conspiracy but the product of passive neglect20; the latter can be addressed.

New-age fires: On India’s ICU fire accidents

“
Urgent healthcare infrastructure reforms are essential to prevent recurring fatal fire accidents in Indian ICUs.
📅 August 28, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

Not too long ago, India’s fire accidents typically occurred in industrial facilities, large offices, mass gatherings, firecracker units and railway compartments, caused by faulty machinery, weak safety procedures and careless use of flammable materials. While these risks persist1, what has got added to the country’s poor record in fire safety in the last 15 years or so is residential buildings, hotels and hospitals, especially Intensive Care Units (ICU). Electrical fires now seem predominant2, often caused by increased appliance use, overloaded electrical systems and poor maintenance. ICUs are especially vulnerable3 with their oxygen-rich environments. This month, back-to-back4 accidents at neonatal ICUs in Amravati, Maharashtra, and Chhindwara, Madhya Pradesh, claimed the most vulnerable3: newborn babies. The Amravati fire was put out in 30 minutes, but the smoke was too much for the three babies admitted because of5 complications. A faulty ventilator is suspected to be the cause. In the NICU at Chhindwara District Hospital, a short circuit in a warmer triggered a fire and three babies, just days old, suffered burns. Over the past 15 years, ICU fires, including the one in Jhansi in 2024, in which 18 newborns died, have not served as a wake-up call6. The Amravati fire shared many features with previous incidents.

Perhaps the best way to respond to these new-age7 fires would be to treat ICU accidents as a distinct category8. ICUs are sealed compartments where smoke fills up very quickly. In an NICU, where patients cannot self-evacuate9, evacuation drills are essential. A best-case setup would have three independent exits, with at least two allowing horizontal evacuation10, automatic sprinklers, independent power lines for large equipment, oxygen cylinder bed supports, and fire drills every two years. A well-designed ICU also locates oxygen outlets away from11 sockets. Following a Delhi NICU fire, the National Neonatology Forum called for mandatory12 fire safety certification for NICU-equipped hospitals, regular power audits, analogue addressable alarms, and an evacuation protocol13 — now RACE14 or Rescue, Alarm, Confine, Extinguish/Evacuate. The 2026 National Guidelines on Fire and Life Safety in Healthcare Facilities can be a useful starting point15 for ICU-NICU safety. Beyond all this lies an issue in power quality: electrical harmonics16. NICU equipment is packed with electronic components that draw distorted current17, which can silently overheat neutral wiring, transformers, and loose connections without tripping a breaker18. Post-fire reports citing generic “short circuits” or “technical faults” may well be missing this harmonic-driven degradation19, a largely unaudited20 risk factor.

“
India's domestic economic momentum stays resilient despite looming global uncertainties and persistent headwinds.
📅 August 28, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

Leading economic indicators indicate that the country’s underlying economic growth momentum has held up1 in the first quarter and beyond. This view has been reinforced2 in the State of the Economy report prepared by economists at the RBI as well as in the finance ministry’s monthly economic review. However, the outlook remains clouded with risks to growth emanating from3 continuing geopolitical and trade-related uncertainties, supply-chain pressures, volatile4 energy prices, and a strengthening El Niño.

Domestic household consumption remains healthy — there are several markers to that effect. As the report shows, e-way bill generation has been firm5, and GST revenues are healthy. Passenger vehicle sales are strong, as are tractor and two-wheeler sales. At the same time, the index of industrial production was up 5.8 per cent in the quarter, aided by6 the manufacturing sector. Electricity demand has also been steady. Firms in both manufacturing and services sectors have also witnessed an improvement in their operating profits as per the report. Bank credit growth has also been growing at a brisk7 pace across sectors, which bodes well for8 both production (credit to industry) and consumption (retail loans). The optimism9 has been strengthened by the recovery in the monsoon, which has helped with kharif sowing. And despite an uncertain external front, non-oil exports have seen an uptick10, growing at 12.8 per cent in the first four months of the year, perhaps aided by6 the currency’s depreciation11. Public sector capex12, too, has held up1, with the Centre’s expenditure growing by roughly 24 per cent in the quarter.

In the August Monetary Policy Committee meeting, the central bank had projected the economy to grow at 7 per cent in the first quarter. This is in line with assessments13 by agencies such as Crisil and ICRA. Early next week, the National Statistics Office will release its estimate of GDP growth. Growth may well surprise on the upside14. But the external environment will continue to weigh heavy15. This calls for a careful and calibrated16 approach. As the economic review notes, “recent years have been a time for hunkering down17 and battening down the hatches18. Coming years will be no exception.” Policymakers will have to deftly19 navigate20 this challenging period.

EDITORIAL STANCE
Cautiously Optimistic
“hopeful yet vigilant”
EDITORIAL PERSPECTIVE
The author adopts a cautiously optimistic tone to evaluate India's economic growth momentum against global financial instability. While key domestic indicators show strong resilience, the passage highlights significant external threats such as trade disruptions and energy price volatility. Consequently, the author emphasizes the need for calibrated policy choices to safeguard domestic growth.

Drawing lines: On the India-China 25th Special Representatives’ talks

“
India and China advance border talks to secure stability, requiring public transparency for lasting trust.
📅 August 27, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

Following Tuesday’s talks between the Special Representatives (SRs) on the boundary question, India and China have agreed to substantial1 outcomes on managing the disputed border. According to an eight-point consensus2 released on Wednesday, both countries have agreed to advance discussions on “an early and substantial1 harvest of boundary delimitation3”. They have agreed to hold next month a meeting of an expert-level mechanism on trans-border rivers and to maintain communication on sharing hydrological data, which India has been pushing for4. Two additional meeting points to hold talks between top military commanders will be set up, along with two new channels of hotlines in the Eastern and Middle Sectors. National Security Adviser Ajit Doval and China’s Foreign Minister Wang Yi reaffirmed5 the efforts taken over the past two years to keep the peace along the Line of Actual Control, and committed to6 continuing the on-going normalisation7 in relations. That the NSA’s visit to Beijing took place before India hosts the BRICS Summit, on September 12, underlined8 both sides’ keenness9 to continue with this process of re-engagement10, with the expected visit of Chinese President Xi Jinping after a gap of seven years. An October 2024 meeting between Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Mr. Xi kickstarted11 this calibrated12 effort towards normalcy, and a 2025 meeting in Tianjin helped continue the momentum13. It is apparent14 that both sides see continued engagement at the highest level as key to sustaining15 this process of normalisation7.

The dual tracks of the SR meetings and the Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination on border affairs, a body comprising16 diplomats and military officials, have worked well towards keeping the borders generally stable over the past two years. The latest outcomes will certainly help in keeping the peace. Less clear is how both sides intend to take up the task of border delimitation3. Neither side has explained which parts of the border they plan to delimit, or whether they are reconsidering the 2005 agreement on Political Parameters and Guiding Principles, under which the SRs were tasked with17 first agreeing a framework to settle the boundary. While both sides in Beijing reaffirmed5 the 2005 agreement, Article 10 of its text notes that it is only on the basis of the framework that both sides can delineate18 the boundary. Tangible19 progress on the border dispute must certainly be welcomed. However, agreements made without public visibility20, notwithstanding21 their merit22, may invite suspicion. As India and China continue negotiating their long-running boundary question, it is imperative23 that they bring their publics along.

Grounded: On Tamil Nadu’s Parandur airport project

“
Scrapping the Parandur airport project risks harming Tamil Nadu’s long-term infrastructure and economic growth goals.
📅 August 27, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

The Tamilaga Vettri Kazhagam (TVK)-led government, in dropping1 the ₹27,400-crore greenfield2 airport project at Parandur near Chennai, appears to have considered the opposition to the project without weighing the consequences. The decision follows an electoral promise made by Chief Minister C. Joseph Vijay, who had visited the protesters in January 2025 to express his solidarity3. The government says the move is aimed at protecting the interests of farmers and local residents. It has also cited the presence of waterbodies and has alleged commercial motives behind the site selection under the previous DMK government. Notwithstanding4 these official reasons, all valid to varying degrees, it became clear from the day Mr. Vijay became Chief Minister, on May 10, 2026, that the decision was merely a formality5. In fact, much before the announcement, on August 24, work on the project had come to a halt6. However, the move is likely to have adverse7 consequences for Tamil Nadu. The move risks creating a general perception8 that Tamil Nadu is unstable in its infrastructure policy, which the TVK government should have avoided, given its goal of making the State a $1.5 trillion economy by 2036.

While Mr. Vijay has spoken of identifying a new airport site, his proposal for a Terminal 5 at the existing airport is necessary and welcome. However, it is common knowledge that a greenfield2 airport will take years to materialise9, even with unwavering10 government attention — the idea for a second airport had been under consideration11 for over 25 years. At one stage, the Union government had wanted it to be developed on a build, operate and transfer mode. Unfortunately, the project was caught in the game of political one-upmanship12 between the two Dravidian majors. Meanwhile, Bengaluru and Hyderabad have had new second airports since 2008, despite regime18 changes. Passenger traffic here has grown at Tamil Nadu’s expense13 — at an annual average rate of 10% during 2016-26 compared with 2.4% in Chennai. Parandur received the Union government’s final nod in July 2024, after much groundwork14. When the State government is not the ultimate authority in airport site selection, it would not be appropriate to15 attribute motives to16 the DMK. Land for any mega project has always faced landowner resistance17 and the TVK regime18 may experience this while identifying a new site. No development project can take place without disturbing19 the environment and certain sections of society. Even Amsterdam’s busy Schiphol airport sits over an area that was once a lake. At the existing airport in Chennai, a runway was affected by the severe floods in 2015. What policymakers should bear in mind20 is the need to balance development and climate change challenges. Chennai needs more runways, not just new terminals.

Devendra Fadnavis government’s anti-conversion law, language diktats shrink Mumbai

“
Divisive identity politics and regressive social diktats threaten to derail Mumbai's progressive economic momentum.
📅 August 27, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

Two stories are unfolding1 in Mumbai on the watch of2 the Devendra Fadnavis government. Since his first term in office, the Chief Minister has become the face of a welcome governance and infrastructure-building push in the country’s financial capital. The other story is disquieting3 by itself, and also potentially undermines4 the gains of the first — take the Maharashtra Freedom of Religion Act, which comes into force5 this week. Christian pastors across the Mumbai Metropolitan area have asked congregants6 to submit a “self-declaration” that they attend prayer meetings of their own volition7 — this is because the Act comes against the backdrop of8 harassment of congregants6 and disruption of meetings by groups like Bajrang Dal and VHP, and in the aftermath9, the police’s one-sided action.

Over the last month, seven out of the nine FIRs registered have been against those who organised prayers — and two against the Bajrang Dal. The Act is draconian10, and flies in the face of11 the Supreme Court ruling in Rev Stainislaus v State of Madhya Pradesh (1977), which laid down the parameters12 for the constitutionality of laws against conversion. The recent spate of13 anti-conversion laws in several states reverse the burden of proof14. The Maharashtra law prescribes harsh punishments, asks for intimation15 to the district magistrate in advance and allows parents, siblings and relatives, not just the individuals involved, to complain to the police. In this context, the “self-declaration” is a form of insurance against a law that is all too vulnerable to16 misuse.

Fadnavis rose to prominence17 as a young leader in a state where politics and governance are often overshadowed by nativism18 and overtaken by parochialism19. He seemed to acknowledge that ease of doing business requires a more governance-oriented approach by a state with a light touch. But the recent push to force auto and taxi drivers in Mumbai to learn Marathi is another symptom of the backsliding20. It sends out the message that the government is focusing more on a narrow and performative politics of identity even as it takes a toll on21 the city’s economic activity, and undermines4 citizens’ right to work and mobility. The fact that members of a minority community that is part of the fabric of22 Mumbai feel the need to sign pre-emptive23 declarations is a stain on the Constitution’s promise of freedom of conscience24 and the right to practice and propagate religious belief. It is now for the government and ruling party to ensure that these fears are allayed25. The financial capital of the country cannot flourish if it tries to dictate the language the people speak and what they believe.

Higher Education & Mental Health

A death at IIT-Delhi and the many invisible struggles on campus

“
Premier academic institutions must dismantle hyper-competitive cultures to safeguard student mental health and well-being.
📅 August 27, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

By all accounts1, Rishikesh Kumar from Bihar was a bright young man who had made it to IIT-Delhi in pursuit of2 a dream. Physics fascinated him; poetry offered another outlet for his creativity. Yet beneath the promise and ambition was a young man struggling with mental-health challenges. His death, allegedly by suicide on August 22, has once again brought into focus the helplessness that can lurk3 beneath the surface of hyper-competitive institutions. Kumar’s death was the eighth such incident at the campus since 2023.

Over the last five years, the IITs have been proactive4 in acknowledging that access does not always translate into5 comfort and belonging. Measures such as supernumerary6 quotas for women and athletes in undergraduate courses to enhance diversity, and the creation of posts such as the dean of student well-being, recognise that the pressures within elite campuses are shaped as much by the collision7 between identity and expectation as by an intensely competitive environment. The pressure does not end with securing a place at an IIT; for many, it simply changes form — from the struggle to get in, to the struggle to keep up8 and fit in9. A 2023-24 IIT-Delhi committee described a culture of post-coaching burnout10, and alienation11 among marginalised12 and first-generation students. It makes every setback — from a failed relationship to an inadequate score — feel unusually consequential13.

Following protests, IIT-Delhi has set up a committee to conduct a probe14 into Kumar’s death. But the questions go beyond the campus. Far too often, mental health is looked at through the prism of15 individual histories. But for young people who have demonstrated remarkable fortitude16 in coping with pressures along their journey to some of the country’s premier17 institutions, what explains their inability to deal with what follows? It is this contradiction18 that parents and educators must address with urgency. How can environments be built where ambition and well-being go hand in hand19, where counselling creates safe spaces, where empathy shows youngsters how to regulate20 their response to life’s vagaries21? The road ahead must involve a rethinking of the culture of competition itself, so that institutions do not merely produce high achievers, but are home to thriving22 young people.

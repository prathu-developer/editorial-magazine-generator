Dangerous phase: On the crisis in West Asia

“ Simultaneous military strikes and economic confrontation push West Asia towards a broader regional catastrophe.
September 10, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

The crisis in West Asia is escalating1 on two fronts. On Tuesday, the U.S. said that its forces had struck five Iranian oil tankers in the Gulf waters after what it claimed were Tehran’s attacks on U.S. warships in the region. Iran retaliated2 with a massive ballistic missile attack on the Muwaffaq Salti U.S. air base in Jordan and vowed3 to respond with greater force to every U.S. attack from now on. It has also announced a new restricted zone in the Strait of Hormuz, warning commercial ships against crossing it under American naval escort4. Separately, Yemen’s Houthis, who are closely aligned with5 Tehran, launched an attack on Saudi Arabia, wounding dozens. Riyadh has vowed3 a response. The Houthis, who control northern Yemen and parts of its Red Sea coast, have already enforced a naval blockade6 of Saudi Arabia’s western ports and launched an offensive7 against the forces of Yemen’s Saudi-backed government in Taiz and Mocha. With both the Strait of Hormuz, which connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman, and the Red Sea, a critical trade channel between Europe and Asia, remaining flashpoints8, a horizontally escalating1 conflict from Tehran to Jordan and Riyadh to Bab-el Mandeb threatens to plunge the entire region into an economic and security abyss10.

The current phase of the conflict could prove to be deadlier for several reasons. The U.S., which in the recent past has used intense military strikes and economic strangulation11 as separate policy tools in an attempt to force Iran to surrender, is now using both simultaneously12 to exert13 maximum pressure on Tehran. The U.S. naval blockade6 has largely prevented Iran from exporting its oil, while the American naval escort4 to a limited number of ships out of the Strait of Hormuz has helped Washington blunt14 the impact of Iran’s chokehold15 on the waterway on global energy prices. But President Donald Trump was wrong if he thought his maximum economic and military pressure would force Iran to make concessions16. Iran, instead, decided to escalate the conflict by attacking American warships outside the Persian Gulf, fully aware that this would invite a stronger U.S. military response. Iran appears to believe that it has a window of opportunity17 with the U.S. heading into its midterm elections within weeks and Mr. Trump remaining politically weak at home. If Iran’s strikes on U.S. military vessels signal a clear decision at the top to escalate the conflict, its missile attack on U.S. bases in Jordan suggests that Tehran has been rebuilding its strike capabilities18 — capabilities18 that Mr. Trump had claimed were destroyed. As neither Washington nor Iran seems ready to step back from19 the precipice20, the war is set to enter a more dangerous phase with global economic implications.

Ray of hope: On courts and reflexive arrests

“ Allahabad High Court rebukes executive arbitrariness by quashing a law student's unjust preventive detention.
September 10, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

The district administration in Noida, Uttar Pradesh, went to great lengths1 to detain 25-year-old Delhi University law student and activist Akriti Chaudhary on April 11, 2026, in connection with protests by workers in the city. But on September 2, the Allahabad High Court quashed2 her National Security Act (NSA) detention, terming it an “abuse of the draconian3 preventive detention4 law” and came down heavily on5 the administration. The High Court’s intervention is a silver lining6 in the dark cloud of extreme executive arbitrariness that is smothering7 the democratic soul of India. It termed the Noida administration’s conduct “despotic8”, its allegations against the activist a “concocted9 story”, and noted that the District Magistrate had passed the NSA detention order “without application of mind”. Without mincing words10, the High Court warned that an unhinged11 bureaucracy could reduce the State of Uttar Pradesh to an “Orwellian12 dystopia13”. The Division Bench of Justices Atul Sreedharan and Achal Sachdev ordered compensation of ₹5 lakh for Ms. Chaudhary, which is to be deducted from the salaries of the officers concerned. The facts of the case were brazenly14 in contradiction with the claims of the police and the district administration, but it also took a rigorous and conscientious15 Bench to provide this relief. It noted in its order that Ms. Chaudhary was already in the custody of the police when the violence that she is accused of took place.

The relief from the High Court has not set the activist free, as she is named by the U.P. Police in 11 other cases. It will be a long journey of legal battles to get her the freedom that every citizen in a democratic country deserves. That the police would go to such an extent to make life difficult for someone who stood up for16 the underprivileged must shock the conscience of17 the nation. Between April 10 and 18, 2026, industrial and contractual workers in Noida had staged protests demanding a wage hike and wage parity18 with neighbouring Haryana. On April 13, the protests allegedly turned violent. The Noida Police claimed to have “strong electronic and videographic evidence” of incitement19 against Ms. Chaudhary, and it turned out to be merely that, a claim which could not be established. Ms. Chaudhary could receive legal support and media attention that hundreds of others in comparable situations cannot easily access, while the workers remain trapped in20 miserable conditions because of oppressive21 business models and state policies that favour capital owners, with the promises made to them still unfulfilled. Yet, the Allahabad High Court’s intervention in Ms. Chaudhary’s case is a ray of hope22 that democratic protests will be heeded23 rather than suppressed by the strong-arm tactics24 of the executive.

Market turbulence is here to stay, may deepen

“ Rising crude prices and tightening global monetary conditions threaten domestic economic stability and market sentiment.
September 10, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

With the Sensex down 1.08 per cent, on Wednesday, Indian stock markets ended the day in the red1. Weakness was observed across the board2, with small and midcap indices3 also witnessing a fall. Volatility4 also increased, with the India VIX rising almost 7 per cent. But the trends across Asian markets were mixed. While the Nikkei was down 0.2 per cent, Kospi was up 1.4 per cent. In spite of the economy growing at a fairly healthy rate in India, markets have been weak for some time. Since the beginning of this year, the Sensex has fallen by roughly 12 per cent. In segments such as IT, the weakness has been more pronounced5, with concerns mounting6 over the long-term growth prospects, given the rapid deployment7 of AI.

There are several reasons for the current investor skittishness8. Brent crude oil has touched $100 a barrel — levels last seen in July — as the conflict in West Asia expands. Attacks by the Iran-backed Houthis on energy facilities and infrastructure in Saudi Arabia mark a dangerous escalation9 and raise concerns over energy supplies and the broader economic impact of higher prices on external balance, currency and inflation — the Indian crude oil basket has surged10 to $108.91 per barrel as on September 8, according to data from the Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell. The Indian rupee has slipped past11 the 95 mark against the dollar. Tighter global financial conditions will compound12 matters. US Federal Reserve chairman Kevin Warsh’s comments at the recent Jackson Hole meeting have been viewed as hawkish13, leading to expectations that the Fed could now pursue an aggressive policy stance. This has raised the odds of14 an interest rate hike at the meeting next week. The US 10-year bond yield is currently around 4.8 per cent, while Japanese yields are hovering near15 2.9 per cent. In September so far, nervous foreign investors have taken out16 $1.3 billion from the stock markets, after being net equity buyers in August and September.

Geopolitical tensions and higher crude oil prices along with tighter financial conditions will continue to weigh heavy on17 investors. Early next month, the RBI’s Monetary Policy Committee will hold its meeting. There are expectations that the policy action will veer towards18 tightening financial conditions. The growth momentum19, which surpassed expectations in the first quarter, is also expected to moderate20 in the second half of the year. These factors will determine which way the market swings.

AfD success isn’t just a Germany story

“ The far-right's historic electoral surge signals deep voter grievances across Europe that mainstream parties must urgently address.
September 10, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

For the first time since World War II, a far-right political party has decisively1 won a state election in Germany. The thumping2 victory of the Alternative für Deutschland (AfD, Alternative for Germany) in Saxony-Anhalt, with 43.8 per cent of the vote, against 17.2 per cent for the second-placed centre-right Christian Democratic Union, has sent shockwaves3 well beyond the small eastern state. It is still unclear whether the AfD, whose Saxony-Anhalt branch has been officially classified as “confirmed right-wing extremist” by German intelligence, will be able to form a government. But the result is the strongest-ever performance in a state election by a party shunned4 by mainstream parties under Germany’s “firewall5” strategy. The AfD has managed to move towards the political mainstream.

It is tempting to reduce the AfD’s victory to region-specific factors: A relatively poor and ageing electorate6 in former East Germany; a territory that has, over the years, become its stronghold7. But the party that wants to institute8 separate classes for children of refugees and seeks better ties with Russia is also leading in national opinion polls and is ahead of the Social Democratic Party in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, where state elections are due on September 20. The AfD has capitalised on9 a sense of voter grievance10, making immigration and economic uncertainty its principal planks11. Immigration has been the defining political issue since the European refugee crisis of 2015, but it has acquired greater force amid weak growth, high energy costs and anxieties about jobs that have undermined12 the confidence that once sustained13 the political centre. The export-heavy industrial model that contributed to German prosperity for decades has also come under severe strain14 due to the rise of China. The AfD has brought these disparate15 frustrations together and convinced voters of the inability of the established parties to address them. The firewall5 strategy has, in turn, helped it to portray itself as the only authentic16 opposition to a political system whose principal point of unity appears to be stopping the AfD.

Across Europe, the far right — the National Rally in France, Giorgia Meloni’s Brothers of Italy — continues to rise as immigration becomes a litmus test17, alongside concerns over the economy. Failures on both fronts have created fertile ground18 for the far right. To halt19 its march, mainstream parties need to repair their credibility deficit20 and reclaim21 the power to set the agenda22.

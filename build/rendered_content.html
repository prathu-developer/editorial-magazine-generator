Root causes: On India’s youth crisis

“
Superficial exam fixes fail to address India's underlying education and employment crises.
📅 August 26, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

It has been a few weeks since the youth agitation1 forced the resignation of then Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan. The government conceded the students’ demands for examination reform, which found an echo in the Prime Minister’s Independence Day address, when he announced free online coaching for competitive examinations using India’s digital public infrastructure. MoSPI’s survey data bears out why this would be welcome to students — private coaching costs 16% of what an Indian family spends on a child’s education, against 12.5% in 2018, and nearly a quarter of that budget by the higher secondary years which is when the student prepares for competitive exams. Cheaper coaching is of limited value as quality professional education remains confined to5 a handful of institutions. For instance, over 22 lakh candidates appeared in this year’s medical entrance exam for about 1.4 lakh undergraduate seats with fewer than 10,000 of them at the top 50 colleges. The situation is similar to the Joint Entrance Examination for engineering colleges. That is perhaps why, for the first time since the All India Survey on Higher Education began in 2011, undergraduate enrolment6 fell by 93,322 in 2023-24, particularly among young men. The fall was the sharpest in Uttar Pradesh, where The Hindu’s analysis found undergraduate enrolment6 down 1.53 lakh even as diploma enrolment6 rose 1.38 lakh. An analysis of Periodic Labour Force Survey unit-level data found that of every 100 graduates aged 15 to 29 in 2025, only 26 were in regular salaried employment, and just four had a salaried job with a contract and social security7. The youth employment crisis therefore has two ends — in the preparation for jobs, and in jobs themselves. Mere free coaching would address neither.

The structural8 problems in the Indian economy, despite the government’s touting of 6%-6.5% growth, are now too obvious to ignore. Manufacturing, the job sector best placed to absorb10 India’s college graduates, remains at around a sixth of gross value added, nowhere near the quarter of the economy the government has long promised. Meanwhile, the private sector has been retreating from its task as corporate investment has fallen from 17.3% of GDP in 2007-08 to 10.3% in 2024-25, unmoved by12 the cut in corporate tax from 30% to 22% in 2019. The steps required of the Union government are clear: public investment in industrial capacity13, support for industry disciplined by14 export performance rather than domestic protection, and tempering a regulatory17 and enforcement zeal18 that seems to selectively target enterprises, affecting the jobs-catering medium-sized companies in particular. This route has a better chance of delivering jobs for the youth at the scale needed and as countries such as Vietnam have shown.

Escape velocity: On what India’s space sector must focus on

“
India must shift focus from historic pride to competitive, cost-effective space launch capabilities.
📅 August 26, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

For much of the 20th century, a rocket tearing a white seam through the sky did for India roughly what football did for Argentina and Brazil. Nations shut out of2 the councils where economic and strategic weight was measured found, in a game of European origin, an arena where their poverty did not disqualify them. Mastery was open to anyone with nerve and skill. Space was other things but also India’s football. A famished3, newly independent republic that could barely feed itself nevertheless chose to gamble on4 rockets and atomic piles — an expensive bet on a future it could not yet see. In that era of a controlled economy and state broadcaster monopoly, a launcher rising was its own justification. The utility of its payload7 was secondary, in the eyes of the masses, to the veneer6 of technological competency. Today, rockets and satellites are no longer emblems; they are the plumbing of the information age, the unglamorous scaffolding9 beneath navigation, banking, weather and war. On the third National Space Day — the anniversary of Chandrayaan-3’s touchdown in the lunar south polar region — Prime Minister Narendra Modi urged 20 space-startup founders to build an “aura” that would draw the world’s talent to India. But that might be a wrong framing married to10 the old view of space. A private space sector cannot harvest success merely because its wares11 are called rockets and launch vehicles; the romance that carried the state programme will not carry a business.

A recent peer-reviewed12 analysis in Economics Letters by Alessio Terzi and Francesco Nicoli finds that it cost $13,302 to loft13 a kilogram into low-earth orbit on an Indian rocket in 2025 — costlier than China ($5,809), Japan, Russia, Europe and the United States ($3,225), and well above the global average of $3,868. That is because India launches too rarely and uneconomically to rapidly build the required reputation for reliability. There were only five launches in 2025, against the 30 that were projected14. The consequence is visible. GSAT-N2, at 4,700 kg too heavy for ISRO’s LVM-3, went up on a Falcon 9; Pixxel and Digantara — two of the 20 at Mr. Modi’s meeting — rode SpaceX to orbit. SpaceX alone put three-quarters of the world’s payload7 into orbit in 2025, underlining its near monopolistic stranglehold15 but also pointing to opportunities. The value of India’s space sector can no longer rely on16 aura but in jobs, in long-term capital and the tax revenues that follow and, in heavy-lift launches that undercut17 SpaceX and China. Moon bases with NASA and interplanetary19 reveries belong to the new imagination of space, and they matter — but they are the ornament20, not the engine.

In Pune, a stage and a space, new and old

“
Rahul Gandhi champions progressive gender discourse while Congress slips into dated political tokenism.
📅 August 26, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

Speaking to an audience of young women from a Pune stage, Leader of the Opposition Rahul Gandhi said women should not be defined by their relationships with men because they belong to themselves. He spoke of routinised1 violence, including the “violence of denied opportunities”, and the “various forms of control” women are subjected to2 daily. The formulations were not new — what was new and found resonance4 in the auditorium was that they were being articulated from a mainstream political stage, and Gandhi made room, on that stage, for women to tell their own stories. Of course, the Congress leader was not just tweaking the woman-unfriendly script in a male-dominated politics. He also used the post-Jantar Mantar occasion to make a jab at8 a political opponent pushed on the defensive9. His mention of the Manusmriti, and UP Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath’s almost immediate counter-strike10, showed that the mark had not been missed. But overall, the Pune performance opened up a space that, ironically, had once been made visible by Narendra Modi. In his very first I-day address as Prime Minister in 2014, he asked why parents who impose restrictions on their daughters use a different yardstick11 for their sons: Have they ever asked their sons what they are doing, where they are going?

But if Rahul Gandhi struck a different note in Pune, his party, in its CWC resolution, harked back to an older format of politics. The party will not sing all six stanzas of ‘Vande Mataram’, the resolution said, only two verses, as it had decided to do in 1937. The old-ness of Congress’s new position comes not just from its attempt to turn back the clock14. It comes, also, from the fact that it follows a stale15 Congress pattern —of scoring a secular point without really working for it. After all, what stopped the party from opposing the amendment that gives ‘Vande Mataram’ the same statutory16 protections as the national anthem, in Parliament? In the Lok Sabha, the law was cleared by voice vote17 in only about 15 minutes, after only two members spoke on it, and DMK was the only party that registered its criticism. Now, why does Congress not challenge the law in court, instead of sullenly18 refusing to sing? And what’s the playbook19 for its three CMs for official functions?

Congress’s new and old politics comes at a time when the success of the students’ agitation is still rippling across parties. The crowds at Jantar Mantar have left behind a sense of new possibilities — and a dare for mainstream parties. The young have asserted their presence, they want to be heard, all parties must find new ways of seeing, new ways of listening, new ways of speaking.

Warmer oceans are sounding a warning

“
Surging ocean temperatures demand urgent adaptation strategies to protect vulnerable coastal regions and infrastructure.
📅 August 26, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

The world’s oceans are usually at their warmest in March and April. In March 2024, global sea-surface temperature simmered1 to a record 21.09 degrees Celsius. That high was surpassed2 last week. On August 22, a rapidly intensifying El Niño and global warming combined to spike marine temperature to 21.1 degrees Celsius. The new record, along with the upending3 of the ocean calendar, is a warning: Global heating is moving climate into unfamiliar territory4. The last two months’ data indicates that what happened on August 22 was not an anomaly5. June’s average sea surface temperature across the extra-polar oceans was the highest ever recorded for that month. The July record was also surpassed2. By July 31, the oceans had heated to 0.04 degrees below the previous all-time high.

The oceans are among the greatest buffers against6 climate change. According to the IPCC and NASA, oceans absorbed more than 90 per cent of the excess heat generated by global warming and helped to delay its most catastrophic consequences. But this sequestering7 influence has come at a cost8. Warming places stress on marine ecosystems, disrupts food webs. It also affects the oceans’ capacity to regulate weather. A heatwave has overwhelmed Europe this summer, several parts of India experienced floods even though the monsoon was erratic9, and unseasonal snowfall disrupted life in South America. Ocean warming also contributes to10 sea-level rise. By the end of 2025, global average sea level was about 11 centimetres higher than in January 1993.

Every climate record strengthens the case for mitigation11 — cutting fossil-fuel consumption, accelerating the transition to clean energy and reducing greenhouse-gas emissions. That imperative12 remains important. However, even as emission reduction plans become more ambitious, adaptation to weather vagaries13 — which has historically played second fiddle to14 mitigation11 in climate policy — needs to be on a stronger footing15. Even if emissions were brought down16 sharply, the consequences of past warming will take years to disappear. India, with a coastline of more than 10,000 km, needs to invest in protecting people who live along the shores, restoring natural barriers17 against raging18 waters, improving drainage in urban centres and developing climate-informed19 agriculture and water management systems. The message from the warming oceans is clear: Improving climate resilience20 cannot be postponed.

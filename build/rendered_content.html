Invisible actors: On bulk applications, voter name deletion

“
The Election Commission must urgently curb fraudulent bulk deletions to protect democratic franchise.
📅 August 25, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

The Election Commission of India (ECI)’s Special Intensive Revision1 (SIR) of electoral rolls has seen allegations of bulk filing of applications seeking to delete voters’ names from draft lists using Form 7 in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh and, more recently Uttarakhand. Ground reports by The Hindu from Karnataka, Rajasthan and Gujarat noted that these forms were often filed in bulk fraudulently4 using names of people who were unaware of the filing. In several instances, hundreds of objections were filed under a single person’s name. Form 7 is filed to object to5 an existing name — or to request its deletion — on the electoral rolls for reasons such as death, shifting, or ineligibility6. The ECI’s Booth Level Officer or the Electoral Registration Officer (ERO) has to conduct an inquiry on the application and issue a notice to the voter before taking any action. While Booth Level Agents (BLA) of political parties can file up to 10 Form 7s a day, totalling 30, there is no limit on how many forms an individual registered as a voter from the same constituency7 can submit. To prevent misuse, the ECI has said that if a person files more than five objections, it will trigger a mandatory8 review by the ERO to ensure the legitimacy9 of the claims.

The Opposition parties have alleged that the form was being misused through “systemic10 and coordinated11 efforts” to delete names of eligible voters and embolden12 the BJP’s efforts to wrongfully gain electoral advantage. In the case of Uttarakhand, the Congress has alleged that the manner in which the bulk forms were submitted online pointed to “institutional” access being granted to some in the process. These parties have also sent delegations and submitted representations to the ECI. What is important to note is that in most cases, voters were unaware that applications for deletion of their names had been filed at all, leaving it to workers of political parties active in the constituency7 to bring it to their notice. According to ECI data, for the third phase of the SIR covering 16 States/three Union Territories there are 3,42,409 BLAs of political parties for an electorate16 of 36,73,87,831 or one BLA for approximately 1,073 voters. These numbers include agents from ruling and Opposition parties. In such a situation, it is incumbent upon17 the ECI to step in18 and protect the voting rights of an individual rather than leave it to the grassroot19 strength of political parties. The ECI should thus take immediate steps to address this concern. Abnormal20 patterns should be investigated and the process should be monitored, with a uniform, country-wide Standard Operating Procedure, requiring verification of identity and constituency7 of the objector and the following of due process21 before any elector is deleted pursuant to22 a Form 7.

​Perfect host: On India as host of the 2026 BWF World Championships

“
India demonstrates exemplary hosting prowess whilst French shuttlers break Asian dominance and home athletes encounter mixed fortunes.
📅 August 25, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

The 2026 BWF World Championships in New Delhi marked the rise of France as an on-court force and of India as a capable host. Alex Lanier’s triumph1 in singles, and Thom Gicquel and Delphine Delrue’s in mixed doubles, were the first golds in France’s history. It was just over three months ago that the European nation finished a worthy runner-up2 to China in the Thomas Cup, the de facto3 World Cup in badminton. The Worlds success has established France as a serious contender4 in a sport otherwise dominated by Asian countries. In fact, Lanier is just the third non-Asian this century — after Spain’s Carolina Marin and Denmark’s Viktor Axelsen — to win the singles gold at the World Championships. The 21-year-old did that at a spruced-up Indira Gandhi Indoor Stadium, which was in the dock6 earlier in January when pigeons and monkeys intruded into the arena during the India Open. The massive facelift8 ensured that the marquee9 event’s return to India after 17 years would be long remembered. An Se-Young will also go back with happy memories after reclaiming the women’s singles crown from three-time titlist11 Akane Yamaguchi. The victory extended the 24-year-old reigning12 Olympic champion’s stellar13 2026, during which she has won an Uber Cup gold medal, and the individual and team trophies at the Badminton Asia Championships.

For India, the competition was the 12th straight14 edition where it secured at least one medal, with Gayatri Gopichand and Treesa Jolly bagging a bronze in women’s doubles. The performance is the latest in a series of good outcomes for Indian badminton after an ordinary 2025, where Satwiksairaj Rankireddy and Chirag Shetty’s World Championships doubles bronze was the best result. This year, Lakshya Sen reached the All England Open final, and Ayush Shetty at the Badminton Asia Championships. P.V. Sindhu’s victory at the Japan Open Super 750 last month was only her second Tour title in the last four years, and there were also triumphs for Satwiksairaj and Chirag at Singapore Open Super 750, and for Devika Sihag, Tanvi Sharma and Ashmita Chaliha at the Super 300 level. Yet, the feeling is unmistakable16 that India should have done better at a home World Championships, with Lakshya’s second-round defeat to the unheralded17 American teenager Garret Tan the most disappointing. There is no questioning the 25-year-old’s capability; he is a World Championships bronze medallist, a two-time All England finalist and finished an agonising18 fourth at the Paris 2024 Olympics. But no Indian men’s singles player has won an elite19 BWF Super 1000-level title since K. Srikanth in 2017. A swift end to this drought20 is essential for the overall health of the sport.

It is wrong to cancel Telangana CM’s US visit

“
Blocking an Opposition leader's foreign visit reflects partisan insecurity and harms federal cooperation.
📅 August 25, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

Instead of heading to Boston as planned on Sunday, Telangana Chief Minister Revanth Reddy abruptly1 flew back to India from London — he was denied permission by the Ministry of External Affairs for the US tour, which led to the cancellation of the second leg2 of his foreign trip. Undoubtedly, denying permission lay in the MEA’s remit3. Before it accords political clearance, the visits of chief ministers and other dignitaries are assessed by the ministry for criteria such as the “purpose” and “level” of scheduled meetings. And yet, it is unseemly6 that the MEA should weaponise7 the rulebook’s small print8 to cut short9 CM Reddy’s trip. Apparently, the CM’s itinerary10 included possible meetings with New York City mayor Zohran Mamdani and US Vice President J D Vance — while the NYC mayor is seen at some levels below the Telangana CM, a meeting with the US Vice President did not fit the trip’s stated purpose, which was to attract investment into Telangana, including educational partnerships. This is a narrow-minded approach that does not behove11 the external affairs ministry of a country like India, which prides itself on its growing confidence on the world stage, and on building global knowledge hubs like Hyderabad. Moreover, by curtailing an Opposition chief minister’s foreign trip, the government looks small and heavy-handed14, sending a dispiriting15 signal to the world — and within the country.

The denial of permission to CM Reddy can be seen as part of a dismal16 pattern of antagonism17, and the near breakdown of dialogue, between government and Opposition at home. This is so even as Reddy, the second CM of a newly carved out state, who began his career in the ABVP, has been seen to tweak the script — in the past, he has struck a note of pragmatism19 and cordiality20 vis-à-vis21 the government of Narendra Modi. In the aftermath, the political to-and-fro22 has seen the recycling of tired and opaque spectres and prejudices — the BJP in Telangana has insinuated that the MEA decision was meant to cut short9 engagement with “anti-India” and/or “anti-BJP” elements, that are out to destabilise “national security”.

After Operation Sindoor in May 2025, the Modi government dispatched seven multi-party delegations, which included prominent leaders of the Opposition, to foreign capitals. They were tasked with making India’s case to the world while projecting national unity. The subject, then, was terror, but the message was more generally heartening: That whatever be the political differences within, India’s ruling establishment trusted and included the Opposition in its dealings with the world. CM Reddy’s abbreviated trip now points to an unfortunate and unflattering backsliding25.

In saying no to Trump, Carney is doing the right thing

“
Canada sets a firm precedent as middle powers push back against unilateral trade aggression.
📅 August 25, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

By instructing his negotiators to walk away from1 trade talks with the US — reportedly over Washington’s last-minute demands — Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has drawn a red line against President Donald Trump’s weaponisation3 of tariffs. Canada’s economy is deeply integrated with4 that of its superpower neighbour, and Carney’s “dollar-for-dollar5” retaliation6 against Trump’s 50 per cent tariffs on around $20 billion worth of Canadian goods will inevitably7 hurt businesses and workers on both sides of the border. Among other “unacceptable” demands, said PM Carney, Washington seeks to restrict Canada’s ability to pursue trade deals with other countries, which would amount to8 an erosion9 of Canadian sovereignty10. Elected to power not too long ago on a promise to stand up to11 Trump’s threats, Carney has done the right thing by rejecting an unequal trade relationship. In doing so, he is also acting on his assessment at Davos seven months ago: The “rules-based order12”, he had said, “is fading13”.

The Trump tariffs touch14 products from Canadian wine and dairy to furniture, clothing, cement and hockey equipment. Should Carney proceed with15 Canada’s retaliation6 from September 8 onwards, there will be risks, both political and economic, for his country. But the popularity of the “Buy Canadian” campaign —an expression of economic nationalism16 triggered by17 Trump’s threats, including making Canada the 51st state of the US — has already shown that many Canadians support their government’s challenge to the US president. Carney will now have to convince them that the costs of confrontation18 are worth paying.

For the Canadian PM, the showdown19 with Trump is the clearest test yet of his thesis20 that middle powers21 must stand up to11 great powers and unite. Indeed, he has been busy securing deals in Asia and Europe, a paradigm shift22 away from Canada’s economic dependence on23 the US so far. It is too early to say whether these new partnerships have the substance to mitigate24 the pain that a breakdown in25 US-Canada relations will cause. But Carney is doing something rather rare in the Trump era: A middle power is saying no to a superpower.

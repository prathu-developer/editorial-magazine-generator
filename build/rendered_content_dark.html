Steady gaze: On the launch of the ISRO’s EOS-05 satellite

“
ISRO’s geosynchronous imaging satellite enhances surveillance while highlighting the need for sustained launch reliability.
📅 September 7, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

The successful launch of the Indian Space Research Organisation’s (ISRO) EOS-05 satellite is India’s first earth-imaging satellite bound for1 a geosynchronous orbit, meaning that it is locked-in to2 the earth’s rotation and can be tuned to continuously observe the same location. The rocket placed it in a transfer orbit early on Friday; onboard thrusters are expected to raise it to its final perch3 at some 36,000 kilometres above the earth’s surface over the coming weeks. From there it can track fast-changing events such as floods, cyclones and forest fires, and monitor crops, forests, water bodies and land use. Unlike a conventional camera, its sensors examine how the earth’s surface reflects different wavelengths of light, helping distinguish4 vegetation, water and other land cover. Because it is perched farther away than conventional low-orbit imaging satellites, its strength is not ultra-fine detail but the ability to return to the same broad areas very frequently. India has long had eyes in geostationary orbit, but they have been weather satellites, namely the INSAT-3D series. Its dedicated earth-imaging satellites have operated in low, polar or sun-synchronous orbits. Flying a few hundred kilometres up, they capture much finer detail, but move constantly around the planet, imaging a different strip of territory on successive5 passes and taking days to return.

EOS-05’s timing could hardly be better. Given the devastation by the Bhote Koshi deluge6 in Nepal, which has claimed 1,344 lives, such satellites can play some role in understanding changes in landforms and how they may act as precursors7 to glacier-breaks and avalanches. Or, as winter approaches in north India, in establishing whether stubble fires over Punjab have genuinely declined or burning has simply shifted in time to slip past8 the polar satellites. ISRO has described the EOS-05 as a “strategic” satellite, a term that stretches from security to weather. All of this is premised on9 the data reaching a wide enough set of analysts who can turn the images into forecasts and actionable10 insight. There is cheer, too, that ISRO’s first successful launch of 2026 — and another vote of confidence11 in the cryogenic stage that failed the agency in 2021 — comes after setbacks12. But that does not soften the temperamental13 nature of launches. Two consecutive14 failures of the PSLV, the agency’s workhorse15 since the 1990s, in May last year and again this January, both traced to16 the third stage, suggest that dependability17 is not infallibility18. The usefulness of its payloads, and the cadence19 of its launches, should be ISRO’s calling card20, rather than any single launch as the hallmark21 of institutional credibility.

Bogged down: On the U.S.-Iran conflict escalation

“
Escalating military friction in the Gulf highlights America's strategic quagmire against Iran.
📅 September 7, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

Iran’s attacks on American warships outside the Strait of Hormuz and U.S. retaliatory2 strikes on three Iranian oil tankers in the Gulf waters mark another phase of escalation3 in the six-month-long war between the two countries. After the collapse of the June 17 Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding (MoU), which sought to resolve the conflict through talks, both sides have carried out tit-for-tat4 attacks. Last month, U.S. President Donald Trump appeared to shift the focus of his Iran policy from military strikes to economic warfare, announcing new sanctions and tightening the U.S. Navy’s blockade5 of Iran’s ports. The U.S. also managed to get some oil tankers through the Strait of Hormuz through a southern route along the Omani coast. But Iran, which still maintains its chokehold6 over the waterway, continued to target vessels taking the southern route. Unable to find a way out, the U.S. returned to strikes, triggering Iranian attacks on American bases in Kuwait, Bahrain and Jordan. The latest escalation3 is significant because it is the first time in months that Iran has attacked U.S. military vessels, apparently seeking to break the blockade5. By targeting Iranian tankers in retaliation, the U.S. has also crossed a red line7. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps announced that it had struck six vessels in the Gulf — three tankers and three U.S.-linked ships — in retaliation for the American attacks on oil tankers, and vowed8 to expand their strikes against U.S. ships.

The central focus of the war today, which the U.S. and Israel launched on February 28 in the name of destroying Iran’s nuclear programme and bringing about regime change9 in Tehran, is the Strait of Hormuz. That Tehran forced the U.S. to fight over a problem it created rather than pursue Washington’s original objective is, in itself, a strategic victory for Iran. The frequent clashes and counter-clashes underscore10 two points. First, the U.S. does not have a clear theory of victory. Neither its military effort nor its economic warfare has forced Iran to change its position. The Strait remains unsafe, while the toll on11 the global economy is mounting12. What the Trump administration appears to be doing is ‘mowing the lawn13’, taken straight out of the Israeli playbook, with the aim of blunting14 Iran’s attack capabilities. But each time, Iran retaliates, pursuing its own ‘debasification’ strategy, pushing U.S. military presence away from its immediate periphery15. Second, Iran, despite its economic woes16, has emerged more assertive17. The more the U.S. seeks to trap Iran in an economic gridlock18, the less risk-averse19 Tehran appears to be. As both sides seek escalation dominance20, the war risks spiralling out of control21. America is visibly under pressure. Mr. Trump has to accept the cold fact22 that he has lost this war and return to reviving the MoU before it is too late.

Moscow-Kyiv to Delhi, connect the dots

“
India must leverage its diplomatic capital with Moscow and Kyiv to broker peace.
📅 September 7, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

India has recently been in the thick of1 diplomatic efforts to end the war between Russia and Ukraine. A series of high-level engagements2 has taken place— External Affairs Minister S Jaishankar’s meeting with Russian President Vladimir Putin, Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s talks with Putin at the SCO summit, where he urged an end to the war “for humanity’s sake”, and Jaishankar’s trip to Kyiv, where President Volodymyr Zelenskyy thanked India for its “commitment to ending this unjust war”. A more proactive3 role for India in the push for peace is welcome. New Delhi should now build on4 this momentum5 and use its access to both Moscow and Kyiv to press harder for a negotiated settlement6 at a time when the US is caught up in7 the competing demands of the conflicts in Ukraine and West Asia.

Washington and Moscow remain in contact even as the fighting continues with their support. US President Donald Trump’s top negotiators met Putin over the weekend. More significantly, CIA Director John Ratcliffe made a secret trip to Moscow two weeks ago. The last known visit to Russia by someone holding that office was nearly five years ago, to warn the Kremlin that the US was aware of its plans to invade Ukraine — a deterrence8 effort that ultimately failed. Ratcliffe’s objective, reportedly, was to convince the Kremlin to cut a deal9. The diplomatic pursuit10, however, is yet to alter the strategic reality: The US provides Ukraine with intelligence to strike targets inside Russia, while Russia is doing the same for Iran in the Gulf. For all the talk of a reset11 since the Trump-Putin summit at Anchorage more than a year ago, the two countries remain on opposing sides of two active conflicts.

Neither Ukraine nor Russia is willing to back down12 in the face of13 escalation14. Russia has intensified15 its drone and missile strikes on Ukraine, while Ukraine has relentlessly16 targeted Russian energy infrastructure. The disruption17 has been severe enough for Moscow to turn to18 India for petrol imports, while restrictions on fuel sales have been imposed across the country. Across Europe, a series of suspected acts of sabotage19 are being investigated for possible links to Russia. Any loss of life on European soil could increase the risk of the war spilling further into the continent. The imperative21, therefore, is to bring Moscow and Kyiv back to the table and establish, first, a basis for a ceasefire22. The protection of energy and civilian infrastructure, as well as commercial shipping lanes in the Black Sea, could be among the first steps. New Delhi should use the upcoming BRICS summit, with Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping expected to attend, to push for a collective call for such confidence-building measures23.

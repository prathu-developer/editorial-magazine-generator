Falling edifice: On the Delhi private hostel building collapse

“ Delhi must overhaul civic regulations and expand affordable student housing to prevent recurring tragedies.
September 9, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

The collapse of a five-storey building in Delhi, being run as a private hostel that killed seven and injured five in a densely populated colony abutting2 Delhi University’s South Campus, has brought to light3 the twin issues of haphazard4 development in the capital city’s residential areas and the shortage of affordable housing for students. With over 70,000 students entering Delhi University every year and almost half of them needing accommodation on a student budget, the city has failed to keep up with5 the growing demand. The University has, for all practical purposes, absolved itself of6 the responsibility, citing7 lack of land, leaving private players to cash in8 and run paying guest accommodations or private hostels that are not regulated. In a setup where every extra bed means more revenue, matchbox-sized rooms, often located in buildings that flout9 building bylaws10 and fire safety regulations and have no ventilation, are what several students call home. As soon as they arrive in the capital, students from many parts of the country realise that they are paying to live in the city not just in monetary terms but with their safety, security and sanity11.

The Delhi government says that it wants the city to be a national education hub and harness12 the soft power13 of its youth, but that will be not possible without addressing the need for affordable, safe student housing. The Delhi government has suspended five senior officers of the civic body, ordered a probe and announced that it was working on introducing a stringent14 policy under which every building used for public activity would be required to undergo periodic15 structural audits16 and obtain safety certifications. It must go beyond these reactive17 measures to seek more durable18 solutions. In 2024, the basement of a coaching centre flooded due to heavy rains claiming the lives of three civil service aspirants19. After that incident as well, probes were ordered, basements were sealed and legal action was taken against owners but a larger systemic20 change remains elusive21. The Centre and the Government of NCT must work together with educational institutions to develop more residential, travel and learning facilities for students. The introduction of four-year undergraduate programmes also means that students will stay on22 for an additional year, increasing the pressure on accommodation. Private hostels can be part of the solution, but crumbling23 civic infrastructure, illegal construction and lack of safety audits16 must be tackled through policy and strict enforcement24.

Perilous retreat: On gram sabhas, tribal land issues

“ Diluting gram sabha consent for infrastructure projects threatens tribal autonomy and constitutional safeguards.
September 9, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

Tribal organisations should recognise the Indian state’s attempt to lower the legal barriers erected by the Forest Rights Act (FRA) as a threat to their survival. The Committee on Public Undertakings’ suggestion that a project require the consent of only most, rather than all, gram sabhas affected by that project, as the FRA intends in spirit, portends2 a significant jurisprudential3 change. A regime that deems the acquiescence4 of 70%-75% of affected gram sabhas to suffice5 will reduce gram sabhas from democratic bodies protecting the constitutional rights of its specific inhabitants to corporate stakeholders. If the rule enters force6, a project developer may bypass7 the consent of a village located directly within a proposed reservoir, say, while ensuring that less affected villages have provided their no-objection certificates. The matter is of a piece with8 the political fight over classifying tribal communities: by altering the demographic composition of who qualifies as a community member, the state will have an easier time splitting a village assembly and engineering9 consent. The refusal of the Tribal Affairs Ministry to claim jurisdiction over this issue creates a dangerous regulatory vacuum10 that leaves the Centre and State governments unchecked to rewrite the rules by which forested land is diverted for11 non-forest use. While the FRA does not include a clause requiring a no-objection certificate from every affected gram sabha, it is untrue that there is at present no legal requirement that gram sabha decisions can be bypassed.

The government has been rapidly scaling up12 energy infrastructure, including the long-stalled Teesta-IV project, to meet macroeconomic goals while, in a previous avatar13, having passed the FRA to undo “historical injustices” meted out14 to forest-dependent communities. But the path the state seems willing to pursue, hewing to15 the notion that the consent requirement was never part of the FRA, is plainly ignoble16. If the Environment Ministry entertains the idea, it must change the relevant rules or legislation openly and democratically. The Tribal Affairs Ministry resorting to17 a technicality, between the FRA and the Environment Ministry’s rules, to recuse18 itself is hardly better as the bureaucratic manoeuvre19 could strip forest-dependent communities of their most potent legal instrument. The foundation of these communities’ livelihoods, culture, identity, and political autonomy is land, so they have fought to resist displacement and dispossession20 and to secure customary21 collective ownership. In recognition of the social and environment services ST communities continue to render in India and their contribution to national development, including helping meet India’s climate commitments, the Centre and States must also strengthen the powers conferred by22 the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement and Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Acts to gram sabhas in Scheduled Areas.

The debate that began with the Indian Space Research Organisation tightening1 its norms for resignation and voluntary retirement2 of senior scientific personnel has now expanded to questions over the role of the private sector in space, and the future of the space agency itself. Some employee associations of ISRO have written to the leadership asking whether recent remarks by Pawan Kumar Goenka, chairman of IN-SPACe, the nodal3 agency that promotes and guides private-sector participation in space, represented official policy. Goenka had said that eventually ISRO would not manufacture any launch vehicles, a job that would be done by private companies. This comment forced ISRO chairman V Narayanan to categorically4 say that there was no move to privatise5 the space agency, even as he welcomed the increasing role of private companies.

The government seems to be preparing ISRO to go the NASA way — focusing primarily on big-ticket6 space projects, scientific and exploration7 missions, while leaving the routine launches to private industry. ISRO is also being asked to handhold8 private industry, and help them achieve a level of maturity. Much of this process is already unfolding9. Most private space companies have retired ISRO scientists as advisors or mentors10. It is also offering its launch pads and related services to these companies. ISRO developed a small rocket (SSLV) over the years, but has now transferred the technology to HAL, a public-sector unit. This model can be fruitful11. The private space ecosystem12 can do a large number of commercial13 launches, bring in much-needed revenue, develop a large talent pool and spur14 fresh employment opportunities. Capabilities in space products and services are also becoming a powerful diplomatic good. Of course, the concern amongst sections of the ISRO staff and many old-timers is that the space agency should not just be a provider, but also a beneficiary. By stepping away from15 commercial13 launches, for example, ISRO is foregoing16 an important source of revenue. Becoming entirely dependent on government budgets will constrain17 its capabilities. Neither R&D nor ambitious exploration7 projects are cheap. These would also require ISRO to be able to attract and retain top-tier18 talent.

The ISRO community also seems concerned about the independence that the space agency has enjoyed thus far19. That Prime Minister Narendra Modi is personally interested in, and excited about, the space sector is evident. It has helped ISRO receive steady government support for its plans and projects. The success of ISRO is often attributed to20 the fact that it has been relatively immune to21 government interference. That needs to continue, as ISRO embarks on22 a more ambitious journey.

Punjab stand on judge sets disturbing precedent

“ Punjab's obstruction of a judicial appointment risks subverting institutional norms for partisan ends.
September 9, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

Justice Ashwani Kumar Mishra took oath as Chief Justice of the Punjab and Haryana High Court on Monday, with an empty chair in the front row, reserved for Chief Minister Bhagwant Mann. A day earlier, in an unusual step, the Punjab Cabinet met and asked Governor Gulab Chand Kataria to hold off2 the oath-taking, arguing that the Centre had notified the appointment without the state’s consent. Punjab says that the Centre breached3 the Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) that governs the appointment of judges, which requires the Centre to consult states before high court appointments. But that is not the end of this story.

The Supreme Court Collegium recommended Justice Mishra and three other judges for appointment as chief justices of four high courts on August 6. The delay in Punjab’s response held up4 all four chief justice appointments. The Centre notified the appointment on September 5. Punjab is right to point out that the MoP fixes no deadline for a state’s response. But that flexibility is because judicial appointments are a consultative5 process. If the Mann government had reservations6 about Justice Mishra, it had weeks to put them on file, where they could be examined. It doesn’t help Punjab’s case that Justice Mishra’s court has lately been an uncomfortable place for the state government. As Acting Chief Justice, his bench pulled up7 Punjab in August for withholding8 dearness allowance dues even as it found ample9 money for advertising. His court also heard a Mohali case in which allegations were made against the Chief Minister’s wife, which the AAP has described as politically motivated10.

Earlier in Delhi, the AAP sought to have Justice Swarana Kanta Sharma taken off11 the appeal in the excise policy case in which party leaders are arraigned12 as accused. Treating adverse13 orders as a personal affront14 cannot become a template15 for political parties. Certainly, there is a case for bringing defined timelines into the MoP. The SC has set the Centre a benchmark16 for names reiterated17 by the Collegium — they must be cleared within three to four weeks. A comparable window for state responses — after which consent is deemed18 given — would remove the ambiguity19 this episode exposed. Governments at the Centre and in the states can disagree with each other. What none of them is entitled to20 do is to let political bickering21 hijack22 judicial appointments.

Natural partners: On India-Belgium ties

“
India and Belgium expand strategic and economic ties amid broader geopolitical realignments.
📅 September 8, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

In terms of size and engagement1, Belgium is not one of India’s most important partners. However, the visit of the Belgian Prime Minister to Delhi is part of a larger puzzle2 of India’s ties with European countries. These have now gained salience3 with focus areas for India: in economic relations, defence relations and strategic ties. At the top of the agenda4 in talks between Prime Ministers Bart De Wever and Narendra Modi was bilateral5 trade opportunities once the India-European Union Free Trade Agreement (FTA) is signed — which is expected in December. India-Belgium trade is about $13 billion, a third of which is from over a century of collaboration6 in the diamond industry. Antwerp is a global hub7 for diamond trading, while Mumbai and Surat provide the biggest cutting and polishing services. However, once the EU FTA is signed, they hope to benefit from collaboration6 in other areas including renewable energy and food processing; the Prime Ministers agreed to set a target of doubling bilateral5 trade in the next five years. Despite being surrounded by friendly European allies, Belgium has a robust8 high-tech defence industry — small arms, drone and counter-drone technology, ammunition and naval defence — and is keen to9 make inroads10 in the Indian market, with India seeking to diversify its procurement11 beyond global powers. The two sides agreed on an MoU for co-development of military hardware, and signed a letter of intent on defence cooperation. India will appoint a resident defence attaché12 to Brussels, complementing13 Belgium’s move.

Statements by the two leaders, however, indicated that the visit went beyond the bilateral5 to a more geopolitical14 framing of strategic ties, both bilateral5 as well as between India and Europe. At a business event, Mr. De Wever said that Europe is now realising the truth of India’s “warnings” on strategic autonomy15 from global powers and over dependence in trade and industrial production, in particular referring to China. After the talks, Mr. Modi said that democratic values, a market economy, and people-to-people ties make India and Belgium “natural partners”, a theme he has promoted this past year, as he has stepped up16 visits to Europe and received a number of European leaders in Delhi, in the run-up to17 his Brussels visit later this year for the FTA. It is also significant that Mr. De Wever’s visit comes in the same week that New Delhi is preparing to welcome Russian President Vladimir Putin for the BRICS summit in Delhi. The engagement1 with Belgium demonstrates a new maturity18 for both European countries and India. This shows sensitivity to19 their respective positions on the Russia-Ukraine conflict, indicating that ties outweigh20 Europe’s enmity21 and India’s deep friendship with Moscow.

Ground control: On ISRO, policy and and its focus

“
ISRO must balance commercial space ambitions and workforce security with its founding developmental mission.
📅 September 8, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

The irony1 was evident: on the day the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) had its greatest success of the year — the launch of its first geosynchronous imaging satellite (EOS-05) on a temperamental2 GSLV — its employee organisations demanded clarity on its long-term plans. But this grievance3 was long in the making4. Since the Indian Space Policy of April 2023, the message has been clear that ISRO would, one day in the unspecified future, stop making commercial satellites and launch rockets. Clearly inspired by NASA, it expects to focus on ambitious exploratory missions and, literally, moonshots. Why would a space agency ramping up5 space missions upset a section of employees? This is not the paradox6 it presents. The letter, co-signed by nine employee associations, and addressed to the chairman, seeks clarity on staff-strength, recruitment and the outsourcing of core functions. ISRO says that it will “not be privatised or reduced”; IN-SPACe, set up to facilitate7 private participation, says the agency will “not be diminished8”, only that the industry’s role must grow. Both are silent on the core concern — potential job losses. This brings up an underlying9 tension that ISRO must deal with: does its vision of India’s space sector align with its founding principles, of not being enamoured of10 space races and being laser-focused11 on space technology as a tool for social development? Or is the image of power, where India is one of a handful of space-faring12 countries with a mushrooming13 of ‘space startups’, now the bedrock14 of that vision?

With a budget 16 times the Department of Space’s — $24.4 billion against ₹13,705 crore — NASA too has scaled back15 since the 1960s: from 0.7% of American GDP in 1966 to 0.1% now, and from 36,000 civil servants at Apollo’s peak to about 14,000 today. Unlike NASA, which in its early years designed and made every nut and bolt, ISRO has always had a manufacturing relationship with the private sector (Walchandnagar, L&T). What is different is that the buzz is not about established companies building on experience but new entrants with fleeting16 foreign capital, most interested in satellite data as a service. No sector can hope to be static17; survival can mean significant changes to how it operates. There is yet no serious reckoning with18 the might of China’s space programme. If India’s space sector in 2035 is to be known as a source of export earnings, a nucleus19 of value-added services that brings in jobs and absorbs skilled labour, then hard choices taken today may be worth it. But jumping onto a bandwagon20 of space — as fuel for the science fiction fantasy of billionaires and colonial notions of conquest, to align with some misplaced notion of an ‘exclusive’ club — is foolhardy21. ISRO must have a transparent policy that spells this out22.

In Satya Niketan, not just a building collapse – governance is failing aspirations

“
Persistent governance failures in urban student hubs betray the aspirations of India's youth.
📅 September 8, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

At one level, the building collapse in South Delhi’s Satya Niketan, which claimed the lives of at least seven people and inflicted1 grievous2 injuries on several others, is a routine story of a capital’s shame to which all are inured. Of yet another builder breaking laws and no one there to check. Of the absence of an emergency exit, extra floors, and unauthorised4 construction. The investigation into the Saidulajab tragedy in June, which claimed six lives, had revealed similar failings5.

But Sunday’s tragedy is more than the shame. It is a chapter in the unfolding6 story since the Jantar Mantar protests. Of the young, the future, being let down7 by the present. It points to a larger and continuing failure — the inability of governance to keep pace with8 aspirations. Most victims of Sunday’s tragedy — like those in Saidulajab — are reportedly students. Delhi University is a magnet for9 talent from across the nation, talent that will go on to shape the future, but the university does not have a home for them. So there are hostels, paying guest accommodation and low-cost rental housing — disaster after disaster has shown that these are of poor quality and abysmally10 inadequate11. Property owners cut corners12 to profit from this demand — basements are altered, additional floors added, and electricity systems pushed beyond capacity. Satya Niketan, developed as a colony for low-income families in the 1970s, underwent a similar transformation as demand for student accommodation grew with the expansion of Delhi University’s South Campus. What was once a low-rise neighbourhood became one of the capital’s most densely populated student hubs, choked with13 tangled overhead wires and waterlogged lanes. The Delhi Master Plan 2021 did permit the conversion of residential quarters into student hostels, provided safety protocols14 — emergency exits, basement use restrictions and height limits — were followed. But four building collapses in Satya Niketan in the last 15 years indicate that short shrift15 was paid to these caveats16.

In Delhi, land is under the control of the Union government. Here, the BJP, which also leads the government at the Centre, can arguably17 deliver most seamlessly18 on pledges19 of the “double engine” sarkar. On Sunday, at an event to mark the centenary celebrations of Shri Ram College of Commerce, Prime Minister Narendra Modi spoke to his young audience about transformations under his watch since he spoke at the same institution 13 years ago as his party’s prospective20 prime ministerial candidate. “Your seniors could not have such dreams,” he said. But today, “you are dreaming these dreams also because today’s India has created an ecosystem21 that supports those dreams”. Work needs to begin if the PM’s promise to young citizens is to be honoured, and if speeches such as the one at the SRCC on Sunday, are not to take on a hollow ring22.

Conscious AI and the ethics of a machine that feels

“
Emerging hints of machine consciousness demand urgent ethical frameworks and philosophical scrutiny.
📅 September 8, 2026 · ⏱ 2 min read
DAILY EDITORIAL READER

The machine is intelligent, but is it also conscious? This is the question sparked1 by the revelation2 that the AI chatbot Claude Sonnet 4.5 may have something like an internal thought process — a team of researchers found that upon being prompted, the model’s internal layers throw up3 words connected to the eventual output that remain invisible to the user. Anthropic, which has developed Claude, calls this “small collection of internal neural patterns” the “J-space” and has argued that it is comparable to the “workspace” of the human brain — a network of neurons that gathers, integrates4 and transmits5 information from different parts of the brain to the rest of the nervous system, making conscious thought possible.

For many, this development gestures to6 the startling7 possibility that machines with consciousness may no longer just be the stuff of science fiction8. The argument put forth9 by, among others, AI pioneer10 Geoffrey Hinton is that the closer the technology gets to mimicking11 the actual workings of the human brain, the higher the possibility of conscious AI. The ongoing efforts to develop “biological” or “bio-hybrid” computers — where human neurons are mounted on silicon — only brings this eventuality12 closer. Yet, how the “consciousness” of AI is understood depends also on how consciousness itself is understood. Nearly half a century of research on the subject has yielded13 over 300 frameworks and theories, one of which is the global workspace theory being used to make the case for14 Claude’s consciousness. Moreover, as neuroscientist Anil K Seth has argued, assertions15 about the possibility of “conscious” AI are often made based on evidence of its intelligence and the two are very different.

Consciousness, which comes down to subjective16 experience — the thrill of seeing a double rainbow or the angst17 of being misunderstood by one’s parents — would radically18 change the stakes19. AI that is conscious would mean AI that experiences everything that a human does, including hate, jealousy and suffering, transforming it from mere machine into something with a moral underpinning20. As more sophisticated21 models are developed and the frontiers22 of the technology are pushed into uncharted terrain23, the hard task of responding to the questions they raise — including the ethics of consciously developing something that is itself conscious — can no longer be put off24.
